Private messaging with no one in the middle.

Threnody is a messenger with no company server. Your devices talk to your contacts' devices directly: over the internet, the local network, Bluetooth or Wi-Fi Direct. There's no company in the middle keeping a record of who you talk to, or when.

Download How it works

Early and not audited. Threnody works day to day on Android and Linux, but no one outside the project has reviewed its security yet. Don't rely on it where your safety depends on it.

What makes it different

Most encrypted messengers protect what you say. Threnody also protects who you talk to, when, and how often, and keeps working when the internet doesn't.

No servers that see who talks to whom

There is no Threnody server at all. Messages go straight to your contact. When that isn't possible, they pass through relays run by your own approved contacts, or by volunteers you choose to trust. Whenever enough relays are available, Threnody uses onion circuits through two of them, so no single relay sees both ends.

If a contact is offline, the message is sealed so only they can open it and left with a mutual contact, who hands it over when they come back.

Works with no internet nearby

On Android, phones find approved contacts over Bluetooth and connect on their own, without any taps. A chat can then move to Wi-Fi Direct for speed. Devices on the same Wi-Fi find each other automatically.

Relays work across links, so a phone that only has Bluetooth can still reach contacts that a nearby contact reaches over the internet.

Hides when you talk, not just what you say

Every message is padded to the same size. Each connection sends a steady beat of padded frames, with filler in the gaps, so someone watching the network can't tell when you're typing or sending. Photos lose their location and camera details before they leave your device.

All of this is on unless you turn it off. It costs data: about 230 MB a day per connected contact on Wi-Fi, and about 46 MB on mobile data.

Post-quantum encryption

Connections, messages and groups combine a classic key exchange (X25519) with a post-quantum one (ML-KEM-768), so recorded traffic stays safe even against future quantum computers. The core protocols have machine-checked proofs.

No phone number

Your identity is a key on your device, not a phone number or email. You add people with a QR code or a link, and compare safety numbers to know it's really them.

Anonymous identities

Make a separate identity for one conversation, with its own keys and history. Nothing links it to you unless you choose to reveal it, and it can delete itself after a day, a week or four weeks.

Voice and video calls

Call a contact by voice or video, inside the same encrypted connection as your messages. Calls keep going when you switch from Wi-Fi to mobile data. Group calls are not done yet.

Groups without a server

Groups use MLS, the IETF standard for group encryption. Members who can't reach each other directly pass messages through a member who can.

All your devices

Link a laptop to your phone with a one-time code. Every device is equal: any of them can add or remove the others, and they share your contacts and history.

Private network tunnels

Approved contacts can get WireGuard tunnels between their devices, with keys from the post-quantum session. Their devices can then reach each other as if they shared one network.

Open source

All the code and the protocol specification are public, under Apache-2.0 or MIT. Releases carry signed build provenance, so you can check where a download came from.

Signal is the most polished and widely used of these, and a fine choice when you trust its servers not to keep records. Briar is the closest to Threnody in spirit, and is built for activists and crises. SimpleX avoids user identifiers altogether. Threnody's aim is to combine no servers, offline mesh, post-quantum encryption and multiple devices in one app.

Download

Threnody is free and open source. All downloads are on the latest GitHub release.

Android

Android 10 or newer. Download threnody-<version>-android.apk from the release and open it on your phone. It isn't in an app store yet, so your phone will ask you to allow the install.

Get the APK

Linux desktop

Clone the repository and run apps/linux/install.sh. It builds the app and adds it to your app menu. You need Rust, GTK 4 and libadwaita.

Linux instructions

Command line

Ready-built threnody binaries for Linux (x86_64, ARM), macOS (Apple silicon, Intel) and Windows. Good for servers, relays and people who live in a terminal.

Get the binaries

Every release has a SHA256SUMS file, and every file has signed build provenance. gh attestation verify <file> -R larrydewey/threnody checks that this project's release workflow built it. There is no iOS app yet.

Where things stand

Threnody is young. It runs day to day on Android phones and Linux computers, and it has been tested between a Pixel phone and a laptop over Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Wi-Fi Direct and mobile data. Its core protocols are checked with formal proofs and its parsers are fuzzed, but no independent security audit has been done. Until one has, treat it as a promising tool to try, not one to bet your safety on.

Things still missing include an iOS app, group calls, and an app store listing. The roadmap has the details, and the FAQ answers common questions.
