# Threnody for Android (sample)

An app on [`threnody-ffi`](../../crates/threnody-ffi), built on the platform with Material Components and AndroidX AppCompat and Core, and no Google Play Services. It runs a node in the app's private storage and listens on port 7450. The overflow menu (⋮) groups its settings under *Account & Identity*, *Privacy & Security*, *Network & Connectivity*, *Messaging* and *Advanced*.

- **Conversations.** A list of contacts and groups with their last message, unread counts, and whether each contact is connected. A contact's devices share one conversation. Pending group invitations come first. Tabs show all conversations, chats or groups, and folders of your own (a name and emoji, the chats in it or rules for which belong, when it notifies, and quiet hours). Long-press a conversation to pin it to the top (drag pinned ones to reorder) or archive it.
- **Search.** The search button in the list finds messages in every conversation, and the one in a chat steps through its matches. The node searches history decrypted in memory, so no index is written.
- **Chat.** Message bubbles from the encrypted history, sending (live, through relays, or sealed for mailboxes when the contact is offline), and files both ways. Received files go to *Downloads/Threnody*, except ones the sender marked *sensitive* and ones in chats whose messages disappear: those stay in the app's private storage, open from the chat, and go with their message. Files are kept in history, so they stay in the chat and open from it. Your messages show one tick once sent, and two once one of the contact's devices acknowledges them. In groups, a count such as "✓ 1/2" shows how many members have acknowledged so far, and the second tick appears once all have. A message lost on a connection that died is sent again when the contact reconnects.
- **Replies, forwarding and pins.** Swipe a message to the right, or long-press it, to reply quoting it. *Forward* sends messages (a whole album) to another chat or your notes, marked as forwarded. *Pin* keeps a message in a bar at the top of the chat. A chat keeps its unsent draft.
- **Send later.** Hold the send button and pick *Send later…*: in an hour, this evening, tomorrow morning, or a date and time up to a year ahead. A bar above the message box shows what is scheduled in that chat; tap it to send one now, edit its text, change its time or delete it. The phone sends it, not your other devices, and wakes itself to do so (an alarm, also after a reboot), so it goes out even with the app closed. A message that would go out more than a day late is held for you instead, with a notification. Scheduled messages are stored encrypted, never synced or backed up, and look like any other message to the recipient.
- **Notes to self.** A chat with yourself, shared by all your devices.
- **Calls.** The call and video-call buttons in a contact's chat start a voice or video call (Appendix Q). Incoming calls ring and open a call screen, with mute, speaker, camera switching and floating reactions. The F-Droid build has no calls (see *F-Droid*).
- **Polls and mentions in groups.** *New poll* asks a question with options (one or several choices). Typing `@` offers the group's members. Each chat's ⋮ → *Notifications* chooses all messages, only mentions and replies (in groups), or none.
- **Lists.** Checklists you can share with contacts and edit together: tick, add, nest and reorder items, or paste a markdown list.
- **Photos.** The attach button picks photos (the system photo picker, several at once) or files. Before sending you can add a message and mark them *sensitive*: the other side then sees an opaque cover, and the photo isn't even decoded until they tap it. Photos sent together appear as one album. Location, camera and time details are removed before sending (*Privacy & Security* → *Remove photo metadata*, on by default), and HEIC photos are converted to JPEG so nothing slips through. Photos open in an in-app viewer with pinch zoom, and an editor draws on them or places text before you send or save them. Received photos stay in the app's private storage rather than the shared gallery; the viewer can save a copy to Downloads. Photos whose messages are deleted or disappear are removed too; ones kept in *Note to self* stay.
- **Anonymous identities.** **+ → Anonymous invite** makes a separate identity, with its own keys, conversations and files. It can burn itself after a day, a week or four weeks, or be kept. Its invite reaches it, not you. Its conversations are marked 🎭 with your label for it, and nothing links it to you: no Bluetooth or discovery, no device name, its own port, and its files kept privately. In its chat, ⋮ → *Reveal who you are…* sends proof, signed by your main identity, that it is you, after a warning that this can't be taken back. When someone reveals themselves to you, their chat offers to add them. *Account & Identity* → *Anonymous identities* lists them, with each one's invite, profile, rename and burn.
- **Volunteer relays and directories.** *Network & Connectivity* → *Relay directories* subscribes to a relay directory by its `threnody-dir://` link; you can also scan it, tap it, or copy it. Every identity, anonymous ones included, subscribes through anonymous links of its own. When your contacts can't relay for you, circuits then go through two volunteer relays, each paid with an anonymous token, so the contact sees a relay's address instead of the phone's ([Appendix P](../../docs/appendix-p-volunteer-relays.md)). *Route through volunteer relays* is on unless switched off. Volunteering as a relay or running a directory is for the CLI.
- **Credentials.** A chat's ⋮ → *Offer a credential…* vouches for attributes of that contact, and *Ask for a credential…* asks them to prove some. Offers and requests appear as a banner in the chat, plus a notification, and wait for you to *Review* them. When you prove something, nothing is shown unless you tick it, and proofs can't be linked to each other ([Appendix O](../../docs/appendix-o-credentials.md)). *Account & Identity* → *Credentials* lists those you hold.
- **Profile.** *Account & Identity* → *Your profile* holds details such as your name. In a chat, ⋮ → *Share your profile…* chooses which of them that contact sees; nothing is shared until you choose. A name someone shares shows once you've accepted them.
- **Reactions.** Long-press a message (anywhere, its text included). The panel that opens stays open while you react: tap any number of emoji to add them, tap a highlighted one to take it back, or pick from your keyboard's emoji in *Add from your keyboard*. Your recent reactions come first. Copy text, Select text, Edit and Delete are in the same panel. Reactions show under the message with counts; tapping one there adds yours or takes it back. Works in chats and groups.
- **Voice and video messages.** With the message box empty, the send button is a microphone: hold it to record, let go to send, slide left to cancel, or slide up to keep recording hands-free (up to 10 minutes). The attach button's *Video message* records up to a minute with the front or back camera. They play in the chat (voice inline, video full screen inside the app), Linux's recordings included, and are kept in private storage like photos. A sensitive one arrives covered.
- **Media, files and links.** A chat's ⋮ menu (or a group's) opens its photos as a grid, its files as a list and every link in its messages, newest first, so nothing needs scrolling for. Sensitive photos stay covered there, and links ask before opening in the browser.
- **GIFs and emoji.** The emoji tray has every emoji by category, with search and skin tones. The GIF button searches GIPHY once you agree to GIPHY seeing your searches and the phone's IP address (*Messaging* → *GIF search with GIPHY*); otherwise it offers GIFs on the phone. The GIF you pick is downloaded and sent like a photo.
- **Clearing and deleting.** Long-press a conversation in the list, or use the chat's ⋮ menu. *Clear chat* deletes the messages on all your devices and keeps the contact; *Delete contact* removes them and the chat. Groups can be cleared on this device.
- **Groups.** *New group* under **+** creates an MLS group that you own. You can invite contacts, see members, and remove them as the owner. Members' messages show who sent them, and files can be sent to a group like any chat. Invitations from mutually approved contacts are accepted automatically. Others appear in the list with **Join** and **Decline**.
- **Editing and deleting.** Long-press one of your own messages to **Edit** it; it shows as *edited* for them too. Long-press any message to delete it for you (on all your devices). For your own 1:1 messages there's also *for everyone*: the recipient's devices delete it too, if they run a current version.
- **Message requests.** Messages from people you haven't accepted (by approving them, using their invite, or writing to them) show at the top of the list as requests, with **Accept**, **Block** and **Delete**. You can't reply until you accept, and the notification says only "New message request".
- **Trust.** A banner asks you to approve new contacts, and to compare safety numbers. Unverified chats say so in the warning colour. If a contact you verified adds a device, you get a safety alert, and **Compare** takes you through the new device's number. The contact menu has rename, approve or revoke, the safety number, disappearing messages and Wi-Fi Direct.
- **Invites.** *My invite* shows a QR code and link to share or copy. The link carries up to four of the phone's addresses (global IPv6 first) and, with *Reach contacts over the internet* on, a key that finds the phone in the DHT wherever it is, mobile data included ([Appendix S](../../docs/appendix-s-handles.md#invite-links)). Both phones need 0.17.1 or later for that. *Network & Connectivity* → *Reset invite links* replaces the key, so links shared so far stop finding the phone in the DHT (they still work at the addresses they carry). **+ → Scan a QR code** (or *Scan* in *Add a contact*) reads an invite or device link code with the camera. The app uses the platform camera API and decodes in Rust ([`rqrr`](https://crates.io/crates/rqrr)), with no Google or AndroidX libraries, and frames never leave the scan screen. The Pixel camera app only offers to open web links, so for a code scanned there, copy the link and switch to Threnody, which offers to add it. Tapped `threnody://` and `threnody-link://` links open the app directly.
- **Tap phones.** With NFC on, the other person can add you by tapping: while *My invite* shows, they choose *Scan* and hold their phone to the back of yours. Their phone gets your invite, yours gets their fingerprint and asks whether to add them, and once they connect you're each other's contacts, not a message request ([Appendix T](../../docs/appendix-t-nfc.md)). Both payloads are encrypted, so a nearby antenna learns neither. The phone answers taps only while your invite is on screen and the phone is unlocked, once per showing; at other times it answers like a phone without Threnody. Anonymous identities' invites and device link codes are never served this way. iPhones and computers with an NFC reader can tap an Android phone too, but can't be tapped.
- **Handles.** *Network & Connectivity* → *Your handle* makes a short handle such as `larry-7K3M-QX9P` that people can type into *Add a contact* to find you without an invite ([Appendix S](../../docs/appendix-s-handles.md)). It is off unless you make one; anyone who has it can send you a message request and learn your IP address. You can get a new code or remove it.
- **Devices and backups.** *Account & Identity* lists the account's devices (tap one to rename it, or to remove a lost or retired one), links a new device, or joins another device's account. The app names its device after the phone's model. Devices in one account share contacts and message history. A newly linked device gets the history so far, and messages sent from a sibling show as yours, "from your other device", with their ticks as that device passes them on. A file a contact sends reaches one of your devices, which tells the others, so it shows on all of them. Photos and voice and video messages from your other devices download by themselves on Wi-Fi (up to 30 days old; never sensitive or disappearing ones), from a device connected directly; anything else says *Tap to get from your other device*. *Privacy & Security* → *Get photos from my other devices automatically* turns the automatic part off. Files that come over are kept privately, never in Downloads. *Back up…* saves the identity, contacts and history to a file sealed under a passphrase you choose, and *Restore from a backup…* puts them on a new phone after the restart (Appendix C, *Backups*). Android's own cloud backup and phone-to-phone transfer copy nothing of the app's.
- **Key protection.** The identity file is sealed (Argon2id) with a random passphrase. That passphrase is stored encrypted under an AES key in the Android Keystore, in StrongBox where the phone has it, else the TEE. Everything else the node stores is encrypted under the identity, so a copy of the app's files is useless off the phone. An identity from an earlier version is sealed on the first start after updating. The key needs no unlock, so the background service can restart on its own.
- **Disappearing messages.** Messages stay unless you choose a timer. Each chat, groups included, can set one (30 seconds to 4 weeks) in its menu, and the timer applies on both sides. *Messaging* → *Default disappearing timer* sets one for every chat that hasn't chosen its own.
- **Metadata protection, on unless switched off** (*Privacy & Security*). *Cover traffic*: each session sends a padded ~6.5 kB frame every 2 s on Wi-Fi (about 560 MB a day per connected contact), or every 10 s on metered mobile data (about 110 MB). Filler fills the gaps, so traffic doesn't show when you send. *Onion routing first*: contacts are reached through two-relay onion circuits when approved relays allow, and messages left for offline contacts reach the mailbox the same way, so it can't tell who sent them.
- **Privacy on the device.** Screen security is on by default: no screenshots or screen recording, and a blank thumbnail in recent apps (*Privacy & Security* → *Screen security* turns it off). Notifications say only "New message", without sender or text, in the shade, banners and on watches as well as the lock screen (*Privacy & Security* → *Private notifications*, on by default). With that off they show the sender and text, except on the lock screen and for disappearing messages, whose text would outlive them. The diagnostics log records who sent what when, but never message text.
- **App lock.** On a phone with a screen lock, Threnody locks with it (biometrics or the phone's PIN) from the first start, also after updating, with a one-time notice and *Turn off*; on a phone without one it offers an app PIN. *Privacy & Security* → *App lock…* switches to an app PIN or passphrase (optionally with strong biometrics, never the phone's PIN, which could get around it), sets when it locks after leaving (at once by default) and when idle (5 minutes), and *Lock now* locks at once. The app's own photo and file pickers don't count as leaving. Five wrong entries make the lock screen wait 30 s, doubling to at most an hour, also across restarts; *Erase after 10 wrong PINs* (off unless chosen) erases everything instead at the tenth. A *duress PIN*, entered instead of yours, erases everything without a sign and starts Threnody as if newly installed; biometrics always open your real data. While app lock is on, every notification is one "New message" that names no one and opens only the app, and recent apps shows nothing. It is a gate on the screen, not encryption: the node keeps running behind it so messages arrive (Appendix C, *App lock*). Calls still ring and can be answered while locked, without unlocking: the call notifications (ringing, in progress, missed) never name the caller while app lock is on, the call screen shows no caller or participant names while locked, and hanging up leaves the app locked. Erasing also ends any call and removes Threnody's calling account from the phone's settings.
- **Erase all Threnody data.** *Privacy & Security* → *Erase all Threnody data…*, after you type `erase`, erases the identity and its Keystore key, contacts, history, anonymous identities, received files (the app's own in *Downloads/Threnody* too), settings, notifications and the push registration, then restarts as a new install (Appendix C, *Erasing*). Nothing is sent: contacts and your other devices aren't told, and your other devices list this one until you remove it there. On Android 13 and later the app's permissions are revoked so it asks again; older versions, and the VPN consent everywhere, keep them.
- **Read receipts and typing.** One tick when sent, two when delivered, and an eye once read. *Messaging* → *Send read receipts* and *Send typing indicators* are on unless switched off.
- **Reaching contacts.** *Network & Connectivity* → *Reach contacts over the internet* (on unless switched off) finds contacts on other networks through the public DHT and hole punching (Appendix N); strangers in the DHT see the phone's IP address, not who you talk to. *Always-on relay* picks a mutually approved contact, usually a [relay node](../../docs/relay-node.md) on a server, that the phone keeps a session with. *Route through volunteer relays* is described above.
- **WireGuard tunnels.** *Network & Connectivity* → *WireGuard tunnels* gives each mutually approved contact's device an overlay address, carried by userspace WireGuard through a VPN that routes only the overlay network (Appendix D). Off unless chosen; Android asks before it replaces another VPN.
- **Diagnostics.** *Advanced* → *Diagnostics*: the node's event log, where the key is kept, and manual Bluetooth scan and dial for testing transports.

The layout is edge to edge and pads for the system bars and keyboard, whether the phone uses gesture navigation or three-button navigation (which sits on the side in landscape). It follows the system light or dark theme.

```sh
export JAVA_HOME=/path/to/jdk               # JDK 17 or newer
apps/android/build.sh --install             # cross-compile, generate Kotlin bindings, build, adb install
apps/android/build.sh --emulator --install  # also build x86_64 for the emulator
apps/android/build.sh --no-calls            # without calls: no prebuilt WebRTC (as F-Droid builds it)
```

You also need the Android SDK and NDK (`ANDROID_HOME`, default `~/Android/Sdk`) and the Rust targets `aarch64-linux-android` (and `x86_64-linux-android` for `--emulator`). Gradle comes from the wrapper.

To try it on an emulator against the desktop CLI, run `threnody run --listen 127.0.0.1:7460` on the host and give the app the invite for `10.0.2.2:7460` (the host as the emulator sees it). For example, `adb shell am start -a android.intent.action.VIEW -d 'threnody://…@10.0.2.2:7460'`.

Tested on a Pixel 8a (Android API 37) over Wi-Fi with the desktop CLI: `threnody run -c threnody://…@<phone-ip>:7450`. Messages went both ways and mutual approval completed.

If Bluetooth permissions are granted, the app listens, advertises a private beacon, and connects to approved contacts it hears, all on its own. Otherwise tap **Start Bluetooth** under *Diagnostics* to grant them and start. It listens on an LE L2CAP channel and advertises it (Appendix K). To connect by hand to a device that isn't a contact yet, a Linux machine can `/ble scan` and `/ble connect`. The other way round, run `threnody run --ble` on Linux, tap **Scan Bluetooth** under *Diagnostics*, type `ble 1` (the number the scan printed) and tap **Dial**. Both directions were tested with a Pixel 8a.

**Faster link (Wi-Fi Direct)** in a contact chat's menu creates a Wi-Fi Direct group and offers it to that contact over the existing session (Appendix L). The contact joins, and the session moves to the faster link. A request from a peer does the same automatically. **Leave Wi-Fi Direct** under *Diagnostics* closes the group. These need the nearby-devices permission.

The node is a process-wide singleton, so rotating the screen or recreating the Activity doesn't drop sessions. On start, whenever a network becomes available, and after an approved contact's session ends (with backoff: 3 s, 10 s, 30 s, 1 min, 2 min, then every 5 min), it redials mutually approved contacts. A foreground service (type `remoteMessaging`, with an ongoing notification) keeps the process alive in the background. It starts again after the phone restarts and after the app is updated, without the app being opened. Under battery optimization Android may stop the service to save battery and can't let the app start it again from the background, so contacts would see the phone as not connected; on a later start the app asks once to be left off it, and *Network & Connectivity* → *Stay reachable when closed…* asks again or opens the list where it's taken back. Instead, *Privacy & Security* → *Wake up with UnifiedPush* lets the app sleep with no permanent notification: contacts holding its messages wake it through a UnifiedPush distributor, which then sees when the phone is woken and the IP addresses of the contacts that wake it ([Appendix R](../../docs/appendix-r-push.md)). It is off unless chosen. Messages that arrive while their chat isn't on screen show up as notifications, one per conversation. Tapping one opens the chat, and Back returns to the list.

On a Pixel 8a (2026-10-04), against the desktop CLI, these all worked: the update from an earlier build (the identity was sealed into StrongBox with the same fingerprint, contacts and history), chat over Wi-Fi with ticks and safety numbers, automatic Bluetooth connection, the Wi-Fi Direct upgrade, a three-member group reaching the emulator through the laptop with receipts, scanning invites in the app and through the clipboard, and redialing after the laptop restarted.

The UI flows were tested on an API 35 emulator against the desktop CLI: invites by deep link, chat both ways, approval, safety numbers, files both ways (and still listed after a restart), notifications, and redialing after a restart. For groups, the tests covered creating one, inviting an approved contact, joining an unapproved contact's group or declining it, chatting both ways and removing a member. They also covered light and dark themes, and portrait and landscape with three-button navigation.

Group members don't need to be contacts of each other. A member who can't reach another one directly hands the message to a member who can, usually the owner. That member delivers it, or holds it until the other member is back (Appendix F).

## F-Droid

Status: **ready to submit** from the first release tag that contains the F-Droid build. The recipe for fdroiddata is [`fdroid/org.threnody.app.yml`](fdroid/org.threnody.app.yml), and the store listing is in [`fastlane/metadata/android/en-US`](../../fastlane/metadata/android/en-US) at the repository root, where F-Droid reads it (title, descriptions, icon and a changelog per versionCode).

**The F-Droid build has no calls.** Calls need LiveKit's prebuilt libwebrtc (a static library and `libwebrtc.jar`), which F-Droid doesn't accept. `build.sh --no-calls` builds the native library without the `calls` feature, so WebRTC isn't compiled or downloaded at all, and Gradle's `-Pthrenody.calls=false` leaves out `libwebrtc.jar` and compiles against a stand-in for its one class `Calls.kt` names. That build hides the call buttons, and its node leaves the calls bit out of its `Hello` ([Appendix C](../../docs/appendix-c-wire-format.md)), so contacts don't ring it for a 1:1 call: they ring the account's other devices that take calls, or say the contact can't take one. A group call doesn't ring this build either, and it can't start or join one. The recipe runs `build.sh --native-only --no-calls` and then F-Droid's own Gradle with `threnody.calls=false`. F-Droid signs what it builds with its own key, so moving between the GitHub APK and the F-Droid APK means reinstalling: back up first (*Account & Identity* → backup) and restore after.

Checked:

- **Dependencies.** No Google Play Services, Firebase or other proprietary SDKs. Material Components, AndroidX AppCompat and Core (Apache-2.0), JNA (Apache-2.0 or LGPL-2.1) and the UnifiedPush connector (Apache-2.0, off unless chosen), from Google's Maven repository and Maven Central only. The Rust side has no proprietary dependencies; two libcrux crates come from a git tag rather than crates.io, pinned by commit in `Cargo.lock`. The only native code in the no-calls build is `ring` and the `cc`-built C it needs.
- **ABIs.** The APK carries only the ABIs `build.sh` built `libthrenody_ffi.so` for (arm64-v8a, plus x86_64 with `--emulator`), not every ABI JNA ships, so F-Droid doesn't offer it to 32-bit phones it can't run on.
- **No dependency metadata.** The APK has no dependency-info block (encrypted to Google's key), in every build.
- **Gradle wrapper.** The recipe removes `gradle/wrapper/gradle-wrapper.jar`, the one binary in the repository; F-Droid uses its own Gradle.
- **Versions.** `app/build.gradle.kts` has `versionCode` and `versionName` as literals, so F-Droid's update checker reads them from each tag (`AutoUpdateMode: Version`). The build fails if they differ from the workspace version in `Cargo.toml` (versionCode = major × 1000000 + minor × 1000 + patch), and so does the release workflow, which also wants `changelogs/<versionCode>.txt` (500 characters at most) in the listing. Each release's "prepare" commit bumps all three.

Anti-features: **NonFreeNet**, for the optional GIPHY GIF search (off until the user agrees; F-Droid builds carry no API key, so the app asks for the user's own). Finding contacts across the internet uses the public BitTorrent DHT and its usual bootstrap nodes, which is a free, open network and not an anti-feature.

To submit: fork gitlab.com/fdroid/fdroiddata and copy the recipe to `metadata/org.threnody.app.yml`, with its version set to the release tag. In the fdroidserver container (`registry.gitlab.com/fdroid/fdroidserver:buildserver`), run `fdroid readmeta`, `fdroid lint org.threnody.app` (no warnings), `fdroid rewritemeta org.threnody.app` (formatting only), `fdroid checkupdates --allow-dirty org.threnody.app` (finds the tag's version) and `fdroid build -v -l org.threnody.app` (the scanner must report nothing), then open a merge request. Phone screenshots can go under `images/phoneScreenshots/` in the listing.

Later: calls on F-Droid. `webrtc-sys` builds against a local libwebrtc when `LK_CUSTOM_WEBRTC` points at one, so the recipe could build LiveKit's WebRTC fork from source (its `build_android.sh`, with Debian's `gn` and `ninja` and the NDK's clang in place of depot_tools' prebuilt ones) and drop `--no-calls`. That is a Chromium-sized checkout and hours of build time on every WebRTC update, so it isn't done yet.
